# Research approach: how children's speech differs, and what helps

A parent-and-therapist primer written to shape the app. It is a **synthesis from general speech-language pathology knowledge, not a literature review**: the build environment could not open the source websites, so nothing here is quoted from a specific paper and no figures are cited. Before relying on any number or technique, check it against the sources listed at the end and with a qualified speech-language pathologist (SLP).

Everyone speaks with small quirks and an accent. The aim is not to "fix" speech but to spot the sounds that make a child harder to understand and practise those in a fun way.

## 1. The rough categories

| Category | What it looks like | Typical reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **Articulation (a sound is made in the wrong place or way)** | A "lisp" on S/Z (tongue pokes forward, or air escapes over the sides); a "w-like" R; TH made as F, D or T | Habit; difficulty feeling where the tongue is; sometimes hearing differences |
| **Phonological patterns (a whole group of sounds is simplified)** | Fronting (K→T), stopping (S→T, F→P), gliding (R/L→W/Y), cluster reduction ("spoon"→"poon"), dropping final consonants ("cat"→"ca") | Normal in younger children; a concern if it lingers beyond the typical age |
| **Motor speech (planning or coordinating the movement)** | Inconsistent errors, groping for mouth positions, speech gets worse with longer words | Childhood apraxia of speech, low muscle tone. Needs an SLP |
| **Structure and hearing** | Distorted S/SH, nasal-sounding speech, muffled speech | Tongue-tie, gaps or alignment of teeth (missing front teeth are very common at age 6), cleft or palate differences, repeated ear infections or hearing loss |
| **Language background and accent** | Sounds from another language substituted, missing word endings, V↔W, TH↔S/D/Z | A second language has different sounds. This is a difference, not a disorder |
| **Fluency and voice** | Repetitions, blocks, hoarse or very nasal voice | Outside this app. See an SLP |

### Bilingual note (English and Mandarin)
- Mandarin has no TH sounds (θ, ð), so TH is often replaced by S, Z, D or F in English.
- Mandarin has three S-like families (z/c/s, zh/ch/sh/r, j/q/x). The tongue spot differs between them, and learners often merge sounds in either direction.
- Mandarin words rarely end with consonants other than n and ng, so English final sounds are often dropped or softened.
- English V is often made as W, and English R and L can blur.
- Bilingual children can have a *mix* of both patterns. An SLP should judge speech in **both** languages, not one.

## 2. When sounds usually develop

Charts vary by source and country. As a *rough* shape (check a current chart such as ASHA's or the McLeod and Crowe review):
- Early (by about 3 to 4): p, b, m, n, h, w, and most vowels.
- Middle (about 4 to 5): t, d, k, g, f, y.
- Later (about 5 to 7): s, z, sh, ch, j, l, v, TH, and R, often the last.
- Blends and clusters (spoon, tree, plane) come after the single sounds.

At age 6, R and TH still being unsettled is common. A persistent S/Z error, many errors across a whole group of sounds, or speech others struggle to understand is worth an assessment.

## 3. Approaches that therapists use (and parents can echo)

1. **Hear it first (auditory discrimination).** Listen for the difference between the right sound and the child's version ("Is that *thumb* or *sum*?"). Children often cannot fix what they cannot hear.
2. **Show the mouth (placement cues).** Mirror, drawing, a model, a straw or lollipop for tongue position, touching the ridge behind the teeth. This is what the Diagrams and 3D tabs are for.
3. **Start from a sound they can already make.** Shape a new sound from a close one (e.g. T → S by letting the air leak out, "snake" breath for S).
4. **Practise in small steps (hierarchy).** Sound alone → syllable → word (start, middle, end) → phrase → sentence → conversation. Move on only when it is easy.
5. **Minimal pairs.** Word pairs that differ by one sound (*sip/ship, thumb/sum*) so the child hears why the sound matters.
6. **Short and often beats long and rare.** Five focused minutes a few times a week is the usual advice. Many correct repetitions matter more than long sessions.
7. **Feedback that is kind and specific.** Model the correct word back ("Yes, a *sun*!"), praise effort and noticing, and avoid making the child feel wrong. Flagging a word as "tricky" in the app is information, not failure.
8. **Carry-over.** Connect practice to real life: reading books together, games, naming things on a walk.
9. **For motor-planning problems:** frequent, intensive, therapist-led practice. This is not DIY territory.

## 4. When to see an SLP
- Others, including family, often cannot understand the child.
- Speech is much harder to follow than same-age peers, or is getting worse.
- The child is frustrated, avoids talking, or is teased.
- Many different sounds are wrong, or errors are inconsistent from one try to the next.
- Hearing, drooling, feeding, or tongue movement concerns.
- Stuttering, a very hoarse voice, or sudden changes.

## 5. How this research shaped the app

| Idea | In the app |
|---|---|
| Everyone has quirks; don't pinpoint one thing | Survey and word flags build a **heat map across all sounds**, not a single "problem" |
| Hear it, see the mouth, feel it | **Diagrams** (labelled parts and per-sound side view) and the **3D mouth** that animates a word one sound at a time |
| Sounds group by *how the mouth moves* | One **colour per mouth movement**, used in every tab (see below) |
| Short, frequent practice | Word cards track about **5 minutes a week**; the survey is quick, toggle-based |
| Start from where they are | **Videos** and **word decks** re-rank toward the trickiest sounds |
| Compare over time | **Lock in** and the **version log** snapshot the heat map |

### Colour system (how the mouth moves)
| Colour group | Sounds | The move |
|---|---|---|
| Lips (pink) | p b m w | Lips press or round |
| Teeth on lip (orange) | f v | Top teeth touch bottom lip |
| Tongue + teeth (gold) | th | Tongue tip peeks out |
| Tongue tip up (blue) | t d n l | Tip taps the ridge |
| Hissy air (teal) | s z | Narrow groove of air |
| Tongue up + back (purple) | sh zh ch j r y | Tongue bunches near the roof |
| Back of tongue (green) | k g ng | Back of tongue lifts |
| Throat (brown) | h | Breath only |
| Open vowels (slate) | all vowels | Jaw and lips shape the sound |
